The four dimensions are task-side properties that predict paradigm fit.
They are re-expressed from the workflow-versus-agent contrast of
Section~2.2.2. Step predictability follows from the locus-of-control and
step-sequence contrast: a workflow needs a step sequence that can be fixed
before execution, an agent loop selects steps at runtime
\parencite{dumas2026, schluntzBuildingEffectiveAgents2024}. Information
availability follows from the way each paradigm acquires its inputs: a
workflow packs inputs into node prompts in advance, an agent may need to
acquire information through observations during execution
\parencite{sumersCognitiveArchitecturesLanguage2024, xiaoFlowBenchRevisitingBenchmarking2024}.
Output ambiguity follows from the structured-output capability of workflow
nodes against the open generation of agent loops
\parencite{khattabDSPyCompilingDeclarative2024, schluntzBuildingEffectiveAgents2024}.
Error consequence follows from the auditability and predictability
advantages of workflows and from the design constraints of small and
medium-sized enterprises \parencite{islam2026, olujimi2025, bandara2026}.
The dimensions are retained as separate axes although step predictability
and information availability correlate empirically, because they generate
distinct architectural consequences: low step predictability requires
runtime control, low information availability requires runtime perception.
The ordinal anchors of the dimensions were fixed in deviation D-028 and are
part of the artifact's constructs; they follow below. The
controlled experiments later showed that the dimensions bind at
sub-dimensional granularity: the four dimensions classify tasks, the
measured sub-mechanisms route them.

\paragraph{Task dimensions and their anchors.}
The four task dimensions are constructs of the framework. Their ordinal
anchors are fixed, so assigning a value follows from the definition of the
level, not from case-by-case judgment.

\emph{Step predictability.} High: the steps and the tools they invoke can be
enumerated before execution. Moderate: the kind of step is known, while the
number or parameterization depends on the input. Low: the step structure is
itself part of the solution and emerges at runtime.

\emph{Information availability.} The anchor is read on the information that
defines the task, not on operational state checks. High: the information is
contained in the input. Moderate: the information resides outside the input
in a known and enumerable store with guaranteed access, so retrieval is a
deterministic step. Low: the location of the information is not known in
advance, so it must be searched for, and a later search may depend on what
an earlier one returned.

\emph{Output ambiguity.} Low: a single correct output, verifiable against a
schema or a reference. Moderate: several defensible outputs whose relative
quality requires judgment. High: soft acceptance criteria, shaped
iteratively through feedback.

\emph{Error consequence.} Low: errors are reviewable and cheaply corrected
before they propagate. Moderate: errors cause rework or degrade downstream
decisions. High: errors change system state or carry regulatory or financial
exposure, including asymmetric cases in which one wrong decision direction
is costlier than the other.

A dimension can take a conditional value. A conditional value is not a
moderate value: it names two opposite modes separated by a known
sub-condition. The transactional action archetype is the measured example:
it sits at the high anchor of step predictability when every action is
decidable from prior reads, and at the low anchor when the next action
depends on the outcome of a preceding action. The routing splits along the
same sub-condition.
