\label{sec:TADF}

\subsection{Design Objectives for the Framework}
\label{subsec:TADF/objectives}
Following the second activity of the DSR methodology, the objectives for the framework are derived from the problem specification and from knowledge of what is possible and feasible \parencite{article}. The framework should be an information system that assists a software architect with the choice of an agentic orchestration architecture per task. Therefore it is framed as a decision support system \parencite{kasperTheoryDecisionSupport1996}.
The performance criteria of such a system depend on the task, the organizational environment, and the decision maker \parencite{spragueFrameworkDevelopmentDecision1980}.  

The objectives serve two purposes and are stated explicitly \parencite{gregorPositioningPresentingDesign2013}. They guide the design and development of the framework (Section \ref{subsec:TADF/design})
and are the baseline against which the evaluation and demonstration are read
(Sections \ref{subsec:TADF/evaluation} and \ref{subsec:TADF/demonstration}).
Table~\ref{tab:design-objectives} shows the eight design objectives in three categories: the decision core (what is decided and on what basis), the decision quality (properties every decision must have), and use and deployment (for whom the framework must work and where its claims must hold).

\subsubsection{The decision core}
\label{subsubsec: TADF/decision core}
The first objective sets the decision space. Every task must be routed to exactly one of four outcomes: deterministic code, an LLM workflow, an agent,
or decomposition. The routing must combine non-compensatory conditions with a weighted comparison, and every recommendation beyond the route must carry a bindingness label i.e., mandatory, recommended, or optional. Section~2.2 establishes workflows and agents as separate orchestration architectures. The choice between them is the heart of this research, so both must be selectable. A deterministic verdict must be possible because not every task needs an LLM. Also deterministic code avoids hallucinated parameters, unstable results and token costs \parencite{bogavelliAgentArchComprehensiveBenchmark2025, gaoSingleagentMultiagentSystems2025,zhouSelectthenSolveParadigmRouting2026}. Decomposition must be possible because tasks do not arrive in a standardized
size. Completeness of the decision matters because misrouting is costly: on identical tasks, performance shifted by between $+80.8\%$ and $-70.0\%$ relative to a
single-agent baseline, depending on the architecture
\parencite{kimScienceScalingAgent2025}. Without an explicit decision space, designers tend to take the first option that seems to fit \parencite{falessiDecisionmakingTechniquesSoftware2011}. The two decision mechanisms follow from the decision-making literature, which separates compensable trade-offs from constraints that categorically include or exclude options. Purely additive scoring can select the wrong option; purely binary screening can discard viable ones 
\parencite{amellerLinkingQualityAttributes2012,
falessiDecisionmakingTechniquesSoftware2011}. Bindingness carries the same separation into the recommendations: some conditions must hold unconditionally, others remain advisable \parencite{hanAADMArchitectureArtifact2026}.

The second objective sets the decision basis. The framework must decide on structural task characteristics that the architect can confirm before the
system runs. A technology or architecture improves performance only where its functionality corresponds to the requirements of the task. A fit diagnostic framework needs detailed task characteristics rather than general ones \parencite{goodhueTaskTechnologyFitIndividual1995}.
The agent literature confirms the basis from the technology side. No agentic orchestration architecture is
universally optimal and selection is governed by measurable task features \parencite{kimScienceScalingAgent2025, bogavelliAgentArchComprehensiveBenchmark2025}.

The third objective binds the framework to its evidence. Every routing rule and every recommendation must trace to evidence obtained under controlled conditions. Evidence-bound justification separates decision support from unsupported opinion. Explanations based on Toulmin’s model provide grounds, warrants, and backing and are expected to increase trust, agreement, and acceptance of system advice \parencite{gregorExplanationsIntelligentSystems1999}. The presentation of advice can influence decision confidence independently of its actual quality \parencite{kasperTheoryDecisionSupport1996}. Moreover, opaque and error-prone advice whose reasoning is inaccessible makes it difficult for experts to assess its correctness and decide whether to accept or reject it \parencite{jussupowAugmentingMedicalDiagnosis2021}. Consistent with software-engineering decision support, recommendations should thus be grounded in models and knowledge supported by empirical investigation \parencite{ruheSoftwareDesicionsupport, arifAIDrivenDecisionSupport2025}. 

\subsubsection{The decision quality}
\label{subsubsec:TADF/decision-quality}
The fourth objective concerns the reproducibility and traceability of each decision. The same confirmed task facts must lead to the same route, and every verdict must be reconstructable from its documentation and decision record. This matters because architecture decisions in enterprise automation must survive review and audit. Unexplained decisions are a documented deficit in practice and traceability to the
motivating characteristics reduces disagreement between assessors \parencite{ruheSoftwareDesicionsupport, hanAADMArchitectureArtifact2026}. Traceability also lets the architect relate the verdict to their own knowledge. The more observable an decisions aid's logical operations and their application are, the better the
user can judge the aided decision \parencite{kasperTheoryDecisionSupport1996}.
Field evidence from medical diagnosis shows the alternative. Professionals without insight into the reasoning behind results either accepted them
blindly or ignored them. Only those who could relate the results to their own knowledge integrated them \parencite{lebovitzEngageNotEngage2022}.
Experts use reasoning traces and justifications to verify conclusions they disagree with \parencite{gregorExplanationsIntelligentSystems1999}.


The fifth objective concerns the value of the decisions. Following the framework must yield measurably better success--cost trade-offs than a uniform architecture choice. Performance impact from a technology means improved effectiveness, improved efficiency, or higher quality
\parencite{goodhueTaskTechnologyFitIndividual1995}. A decision technique must guide toward better alternatives
and deliver effectiveness and efficiency as separate benefits
\parencite{ruheSoftwareDesicionsupport, falessiDecisionmakingTechniquesSoftware2011}. The baseline is part of the objective because decision aids can raise confidence without raising decision quality, and such illusory benefits make an aid of questionable value \parencite{kasperTheoryDecisionSupport1996}. In this research space the stakes are measured. Inference paradigms differ in token cost by an order of magnitude, and coordination structures can differ by $3.1\times$ in token efficiency on identical tasks \parencite{kimScienceScalingAgent2025, zhouSelectthenSolveParadigmRouting2026}. Routing has shown leverage: oracle per-task selection can beat the best fixed paradigm by 17.1 percentage points. \parencite{zhouSelectthenSolveParadigmRouting2026}.

\subsubsection{Use and deployment}
\label{subsubsec:TADF/use-deployment}
The sixth objective makes decisions actionable. A route alone does not build a running solution. Every decision for an LLM task must therefore carry implementation knowledge or explanations. Support for all phases of the decision process, implementation included, is among the performance objectives for decision
support systems \parencite{spragueFrameworkDevelopmentDecision1980}. 
Explenations for decisions matter as much as content and are used more and work better when specific
to the case rather than generic \parencite{gregorExplanationsIntelligentSystems1999}. For LLM workflows and agents, guards on tokens, tool calls, and execution time are part of the architecture  itself and need to be considered
\parencite{yueStaticTemplatesDynamic2026}.

The seventh objective concerns the users. The framework must be usable by practicing architects without knowledge of its rulebook. It must ask understandable questions and keep the effort bounded. Ease of use decides whether an aid is used at all. Its users have latitude to ignore it, so it must earn
their trust by being valuable and convenient \parencite{spragueFrameworkDevelopmentDecision1980}. 
Effort decides how much of it is used. Explanations that cost too much effort to access go unused and relating a system's output to one's own knowledge demands time that professionals under time pressure often do not invest
\parencite{lebovitzEngageNotEngage2022, gregorExplanationsIntelligentSystems1999}. Advice-giving systems also place the user in the difficult role of monitoring and overruling a machine presumed to be more competent than the user \parencite{kasperTheoryDecisionSupport1996}. Software architecture practice underlines these requirements. High-effort methods pay off only in large
projects, and architects do not trust fully automatic solutions \parencite{amellerLinkingQualityAttributes2012, arifAIDrivenDecisionSupport2025}. 

The eighth objective sets the validity scope. The framework must be developed and evaluated on the tasks of its domain, and it must declare its limits. Architecture evaluations in the field run predominantly on mathematics and coding benchmarks, and such evidence can mislead guidance for real work \parencite{kimScienceScalingAgent2025}. Few benchmarks target enterprise workflows, although models struggle exactly on routine workplace tasks with realistic, messy data
\parencite{bogavelliAgentArchComprehensiveBenchmark2025}.
The framework must therefore be grounded and tested on realistic workplace-automation tasks at task granularity.
A decision aid should leave its user with a confidence that matches the quality of the aided decision. This requires the aid to convey its own applicability and inapplicability \parencite{kasperTheoryDecisionSupport1996}. 

\subsection{Design and Development}
\label{subsec:TADF/design}
This section reports the third DSRM activity, design and development \parencite{article}. It follows the view of design as a search for an effective artifact (Guideline~6 in
\cite{hevnerDesignScienceInformation2004}). The third design objective (Section~\ref{subsec:TADF/objectives}) motivates the approach: comparing both execution forms on a shared experimental infrastructure and translating the observed differences into an explicit decision instrument. During the development the trajectory was versioned with an interation log to keep tack of ever change. A task taxonomy supplies the vocabulary and tasks to execute (Section~\ref{subsubsec:TADF/taxonomy}). Task instances and a sandbox environment make the taxonomy measurable (Section~\ref{subsubsec:TADF/setup}). Every archetype is implemented in both paradigms and measured in one capability grid (Section~\ref{subsubsec:TADF/paradigms}).
Deep-dive runs answer the questions the grid left open (Section~\ref{subsubsec:TADF/deepdive}). The results follow and where used to find criteria to build an desicion famework (Section~\ref{subsubsec:TADF/results}).

\subsubsection{Task Taxonomy}
\label{subsubsec:TADF/taxonomy}
The used task taxonomy for the comparison is an own creation and provides the vocabulary \parencite{marchDesignNaturalScience1995}. An own taxonomy is used to combine diffrent task patterns of agentic and non agetic task. It also forms task archtypes with explicit task characteristics so that similarities and differences that matter for the decision become visible. Furthermore it provides structural difficulty levers per archetype and one shared sandbox environment to execute the task archetypes. The taxonomy's purpose is to structure the experimental evidence for TADF. Its coverage is bounded
by the task patterns examined in the selected sources. 

The taxonomy was developed iteratively, informed by the
conceptual-to-empirical and empirical-to-conceptual approaches of \textcite{nickersonMethodTaxonomyDevelopment2013}. It started by forming a landscape relevant for workflow vs. agent routing based on the related Work and the agent vs. workflow distinction (\ref{subsec:Background/related,subsec:Background/workflow vs. agent}). Four structural dimensions therefore opend this landscape. Step predictability asks whether the steps can be enumerated before execution. Information availability asks whether the required information is in the input, in a known store, or unknown. Output ambiguity asks whether the output is schema-bound or open. Error consequence asks how severe a failure is. Based on this characteristics tasks archtypes where formed based on found task in related benchmarks in this space. New archtypes where formed when diffrent  expressions for the characteristics where found.  The used benchmarks for this mehtod were then identified in two steps. The start set came from the literature review (Section~2.4). Backward snowballing over the related work of every admitted source then added archtypes until the ending condition was fulfilled by four benchmarks in a row. This follows the backward search of
\textcite{websterAnalyzingPrepareFuture2002} and the snowballing guidelines
of \textcite{wohlinGuidelinesSnowballingSystematic2014}.  The unit of selection is the task of Section~2.2.3. Benchmarks were included in the taxonomy development when two inclusion criteria held jointly. (a) The source defines its tasks for execution by an LLM-based component at the task level of Section~2.2.3, or its scenarios decompose to that level. (b) The tasks represent realistic activities of enterprise or knowledge work, for example executing
transactions in business systems, applying organizational rules, or researching information. Where a source mixes such tasks with others, only the work-related subset entered the corpus. A source that met the criteria was still not added when it was a superseded predecessor or a variant of an admitted source, or when it added no task pattern beyond the admitted sources. This applies the ending condition of the taxonomy method that a further iteration adds no new dimension or characteristic. Thirteen sources remained and shaped the taxonomy
(Table~\ref{tab:corpus-positioning}). The following development revealed the need to revise definitions, difficulty axes, and dimensional profiles while retaining the eight-archetype structure. Appendix~\ref{app:taxonomy} documents the source mappings and the revisions supported by the development record. This record supports the taxonomy's use in organizing the experimental program. It does
not establish exhaustive coverage of workplace tasks or independent reliability of archetype assignment.

The resulting task taxonomy uses the following eight archetypes, labeled A to H, to cover the relevant task space. The development revealed the need to revise definitions, difficulty axes, and dimensional profiles while retaining the eight-archetype structure. 2Appendix~\ref{app:taxonomy} documents the source mappings and the revisions supported by the development record. This record supports the taxonomy's use in organizing the experimental program. It does not establish exhaustive coverage of workplace tasks or independent reliability of archetype assignment.

\begin{description}
  \item[A. Exploratory research and synthesis.] The task is to find an answer that is neither in the input nor in a known store. The information is spread over sources that must first be located, and one finding often decides where to look next. A typical example is a research question whose answer must be pieced together from several web pages. The answer is short and can be checked against a reference.
  \item[B. Structured data retrieval and transformation.] The task is to fetch records from known systems and bring them into a required form. The
  stores are known, the fields are known, and the result must fit a fixed schema. The difficulty lies in combining sources and aggregating correctly, not in finding them.
  \item[C. Ambiguous classification and disambiguation.] The task is to assign inputs that are vague or mixed to one of a fixed set of labels. All information is in the input; the judgment lies in reading it correctly. A typical example is the triage of incoming support emails into categories such as billing, technical issue, or sales request. The output is a label per input that can be checked against a reference.
  \item[D. Compliance and rule-based decisioning.] The task is to decide a case by applying written rules. The rules exist in a policy document or store, the case facts arrive as text, and the decision is to approve, reject, or escalate. Errors weigh heavily, because a wrong approval changes the state of the business.
  \item[E. Output verification and quality control.] The task is to check an artifact that already exists, such as a drafted document, against stated criteria. A typical example is the review of a customer response. The verdict is a fixed label, such as pass, needs revision, or fail.
  \item[F. Transactional action execution.] The task is to change the state of a system through tool calls e.g send a mail, book a meeting or update a record. Success is not the text the system produces but the
  resulting state. A typical example is closing a set of support cases and notifying the responsible contacts. Some instances can be planned from prior reads, others depend on how a previous action turned out.
  \item[G. Strategic and adaptive planning.] The task is to produce a plan, an ordered set of intended actions, without executing it. The structure of the plan is the solution. A typical example is a plan for a key
  account: which records to update, whom to notify, and in which order. A plan is correct when a simulator can execute it and the goal state holds.
  \item[H. Content drafting and refinement.] The task is to write a document whose acceptance criteria are soft and which is shaped through feedback. A example is a report to a manager that reports figures, follows format constraints, and is revised. Quality has no single reference. It is judged against rubric criteria beside checkable requirements such as required data points and length windows.
\end{description}

\subsubsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{subsubsec:TADF/setup}
The setup gives the eight archetypes one place to run and one way to be measured. All instances run in one sandbox environment. It is built in Python on a local SQLite database and was designed for determinism and realism at once. Fifteen tools form the tool surface (Table~\ref{tab:tools}). Four database tools read, search, and update the CRM and close cases through a transactional call with a documented but unpredictable refusal rule. Four mail tools and four calendar tools simulate an enterprise mail and calendar service; their responses are shaped like the Gmail and Google Calendar APIs, so that payload size and structure are realistic. Two policy tools expose an external policy registry for the compliance archetype. One search tool runs live web search with a per-query disk cache, so both paradigms see
identical snippets across runs. Every simulated call and every cached search call adds a synthetic latency of 300 ms plus or minus 50 ms that is derived from a hash of the tool name and its arguments. Identical calls
therefore cost identical time in both paradigms, and latency stays comparable across runs. Tool exposure is fixed per archetype and identical for both paradigms. Instructions follow one design rule for both paradigms: a natural request plus an excerpt of an organizational runbook, with no procedural hints 
\parencite{yao$t$benchBenchmarkToolAgentUser2024}.

The data layer is one SQLite database with seven tables: accounts, contacts, agents, cases, opportunities, emails, and events. All data is synthetic and generated by a deterministic seed script. The records reference each other coherently: contacts belong to accounts, cases and opportunities reference accounts and agents, and mails and events reference contacts and cases, so that multi-source tasks have a consistent answer.
The mail and event tables follow the shape of WorkBench. The database is rebuilt from the seed before every run, so both paradigms start from an identical state and post-execution predicates compare against a known baseline. Instance-specific data is added on top of the seed without changing it.

The metric set to mesure the runs was fixed before the build-up. It covers the performance indicators that the research question targets: task success and resource efficiency. Two metrics are co-primary. The first is success. A run counts as solved when its result meets the reference of the instance, no matter which path produced it. How this is checked depends on the archetype. For archetypes B to G a deterministic predicate compares the output or the
post-execution state with the gold: the verdict label in verification, the simulated final state in planning, the database rows after an action. For research tasks (A) a validated LLM judge decides whether the free-text answer states the gold fact. or drafting tasks (H), a run is considered solved only if it passes all deterministic compliance checks, including required data points, absence of invented figures, and the specified length limit. In parallel, a rubric-based LLM judge assesses the draft's qualitative quality. Using LLMs to evaluate natural-language outputs is an established evaluation method. Both judges use frozen prompts, and are blind to the paradigm. Before the final measurement, their ratings were compared with independent human ratings on stratified samples covering 20 percent of the scored outputs. The research judge met the preregistered agreement criterion. The drafting judge did not meet the kappa criterion but showed high agreement according to AC1 \parencite{zhengJudgingLLMasaJudgeMTBench2023,gwetComputingInterraterReliability2008}. It was therefore retained as a complementary measure alongside the deterministic compliance layer.

Four metrics are secondary. Latency is the wall-clock time of a run. Error rate is the share of runs that end in a wrong output or in an iteration overflow. Tool-call count is the number of tool invocations. Robustness is
the success on perturbed copies of instances that both paradigms solve on clean input, the same task and reference in messier phrasing. Token cost, latency, error rate, and tool-call count come from one execution log that both paradigms share. Three sandbox choices of the previous paragraph serve these metrics directly. API-shaped payloads keep token cost realistic, synthetic latency keeps time comparable, and the search cache
gives both paradigms identical input.

\subsubsection{Implementation and Execution}
\label{subsubsec:TADF/paradigms}
This section describes how the two paradigms were built on the sandbox environment until they were accepted and measured for the comparison. Both paradigms were built in Python on LangGraph and LangChain, with the models called through the OpenAI API. The workflow archetype s set up as a state graph whose nodes and edges are fixed at compile time. Its LLM nodes return structured output against Pydantic schemas, so every LLM result is
typed before deterministic code consumes it. Deterministic code owns retrieval, aggregation, dispatch, and validation. The agent archetype is build as a ReAct loop \parencite{yaoReActSynergizingReasoning2023}. The same
tools are bound to the model, and the model selects the next step at runtime through tool calls until it emits a final answer. Every agent loop is bounded by a step limit, and an exhausted limit counts as
an iteration overflow.  The locus of control therefore stays with the model, as the paradigm definition of Section~2.2 requires. Both implementations of an archetype share the model backbone, the tool interface, the instruction text, and the logging. The logging callback records every LLM call with its tokens, every tool call, the wall-clock latency, and every error
of a run. The paradigm is the only systematic difference. The repository, its organization, and the steps that reproduce every run are described in Appendix~\ref{app:repository}.

The implementations were developed through repeated build-and-evaluate cycles \parencite{hevnerThreeCycleView2007}. The task instances and the corresponding workflow and agent implementations were refined in parallel. For each archetype, five instances were created at each of three difficulty levels. This resulted in 15 instances per archetype and 120 instances overall. Each archetype was implemented as both a workflow and an agent. Both implementations were then evaluated on the complete set of instances. The review focused on three questions. First, did the paradigms differ in their locus of control rather than merely in the components they used? Second, could the model solve the instances from memory without using the environment? Third, did both paradigms receive the same task text and tools? If the review identified a weakness, the implementation was revised and evaluated again. Both rejected versions and versions deliberately retained were recorded.

The instances are own creations and build with the help of coding agents. Their design follows the task patterns identified in the corpus sources. Each instance is therefore grounded in the referenced literature. For each archetype, difficulty was defined along the dimension that produced variation relevant to the architectural routing decision. Appendix~\ref{app:taxonomy} documents the sources supporting each archetype, their use in constructing the instances, and the respective difficulty levels (Tables~\ref{tab:app-iterations}, \ref{tab:app-difficulty}). The following paragraphs describe the instances and their sources, the available tools, the execution of the task, and the changes made during development. Figure~\ref{fig} illustrates both implementations of archetype F.

\begin{description}
\item[A. Exploratory research and synthesis.] The instances are BrowseComp-style research questions in which several constraints identify one short, difficult-to-find answer. Difficulty depends on the number of sources and their dependency structure. Both paradigms use only web search and share a limit of ten tool calls. The workflow generates all queries in advance, executes them in code, and writes the answer in a second LLM call. The agent selects each query based on previous results. Initial questions that could be answered from model memory were replaced (D-016), and difficulty was shifted from fact obscurity to source combination (D-004, D-017). A no-tool memorization check, equal search latency, and an undisclosed shared query limit were introduced. At the hardest level, each result provides the input for the next search, and only the final value is accepted.

\item[B. Structured data retrieval and transformation.] Here the instances are natural-language questions over CRM, mailbox, and calendar data (CRMArena-Pro structured querying, WorkBench mailbox and calendar data). They range from a single CRM lookup to a customer brief combining all three sources. Both paradigms use four read-only tools: database read and search, mail search, and calendar search. The workflow first generates a retrieval specification. Code then executes the searches and uses SQL for joins and aggregations before a second LLM call writes the answer. The agent retrieves individual records and performs the combination within its context, using at most twelve tool calls. During refinement, difficulty was changed from join depth to the number of sources because only the latter explained failures. Aggregation-heavy questions were removed because they tested arithmetic rather than retrieval. The final version also preserved the external API boundaries while reducing the workflow to two LLM calls and a deterministic SQL core.

 \item[C. Ambiguous classification and disambiguation.] For archtype C. the instances contain batches of three to eight support emails with competing signals (CRMArena-Pro case routing and activity-priority tasks). Each email must be assigned one label based on a document of category definitions. The available database tools are not required because the emails contain all relevant information. The workflow labels chunks of five emails in structured LLM calls and combines the results in code. The agent labels the complete batch in one context. The initial single-email task (D-010) was replaced with batch triage because both paradigms had previously used only one LLM call (D-026). The resulting task was retained as a boundary case: capable models reach ceiling performance, while the workflow may use more tokens because it repeats the category definitions.
  
\item[D. Compliance and rule-based decisioning.] The instances are natural-language quote requests evaluated against policies in an external registry (CRMArena-Pro, World of Workflows, WorkBench, FlowBench). Difficulty increases from one rule to at least five combined rules with precedence conflicts; six of fifteen instances require a decline. Both paradigms use two policy-registry tools, while the database tools serve as distractors. The workflow retrieves all policies in code, uses one LLM call to extract structured facts, and applies the rules through a deterministic engine. The agent selects relevant policies and applies them within its context. The initial version included the complete policy in the prompt, making both paradigms single-call implementations. Moving the policies to an external registry and adding deeper rules and distractor policies created the intended rule-based task (D-024).

\item[E. Output verification and quality control.] For this archtype each instance contains a customer inquiry and a candidate response evaluated against five criteria (WONDERBREAD, Kourani et al., TheAgentCompany). The number of failed criteria deterministically produces the verdict \textit{pass}, \textit{needs revision}, or \textit{fail}. The database tools are available but not required. The workflow evaluates each criterion in one structured LLM call and provides supporting evidence. A deterministic engine then derives the verdict. The agent applies the complete rubric and determines the verdict within one context. An initial ranking task was replaced with fixed gold verdicts (D-011). The workflow was then separated into an LLM-based assessment and a deterministic verdict stage to avoid a single-call comparison with the agent (D-029).

\item[F. Transactional action execution.] Here the instances require mail, calendar, and CRM actions and are scored against the resulting system state. Ten adapt WorkBench patterns, and five were designed in the style of WorkArena++. Three of the latter require runtime feedback because a failed case closure changes the correct next action. The archetype therefore covers both fixed action sequences and outcome-dependent actions. Both paradigms use all twelve available tools. The workflow plans and executes reads before planning the actions in a second LLM call. It records failed actions but cannot revise its plan. The agent interleaves reads and actions and can adapt after each result. The workflow was split into two stages because action planning required identifiers from earlier reads. Service payloads were made more realistic (D-006), and three runtime-feedback instances were added (D-030). Broken side-effect checks were repaired, and the instructions were rewritten as natural requests with runbook excerpts.

\item[G. Strategic and adaptive planning.] Archtype G. instances require plans for updating CRM records, closing cases, sending notifications, and ordering these steps under stated constraints (FlowBench, WorkArena++, PlanBench). A deterministic simulator evaluates whether the resulting state satisfies the goal. Difficulty is defined by the optimal plan length, ranging from three to eighteen steps. Both paradigms use only database read and search tools because the required output is the plan itself. The workflow plans and executes the reads before generating the complete plan in a second LLM call. The agent interleaves retrieval and reasoning within a difficulty-dependent step limit. The original Blocksworld instances were replaced with author-constructed CRM scenarios executed by the simulator (D-012). The final review retained this design and aligned the instructions with the runbook-based phrasing used for archetype F.

\item[H. Content drafting and refinement.] he instances are data-to-text briefs containing a data table, writing constraints, and scripted feedback rounds (WONDERBREAD, TheAgentCompany). Outputs are evaluated by a deterministic compliance layer and a validated LLM judge. No tools are available. The workflow produces one draft and exactly one revision for each feedback round. The agent repeatedly rewrites the draft and chooses between \textit{revise} and \textit{submit}; feedback is provided after each submission. The dual-scoring procedure and difficulty axis were defined at the start (D-013, D-014). The compliance check was later adapted to accept correctly derived percentages. The final review retained the implementation and clarified the definition of a tool-free agent.
\end{description}

After the implementations were fixed, both paradigms were evaluated for all eight archetypes across three capability tiers. GPT-5.4-nano and GPT-5.4-mini each completed two clean runs and one robustness run, while GPT-5.2 served as the reference tier with one run of each type. Extending the original single-model protocol to three tiers allowed the stability of paradigm effects across model capabilities to be examined. All calls used temperature 0 and seed 42. The robustness runs where executet per archtype and on tasks instances both paradigms had solved. They added typos, colloquial or jargon phrasing, numbers written as words, buried or redundant information, an indirect request, or a self-correction inside the request. The final grid contained 64 cells, including 40 clean and 24 robustness cells, and 1,452 logged runs. Two technical incidents were corrected and documented. A search-provider limit invalidated seven research cells, which were discarded and repeated after a plan upgrade. Early throttling also inflated some latencies. Per-request timeouts were therefore added, and latency is reported as the median. Errors unrelated to these incidents remained in the data as paradigm failures. These include one agent step-limit overflow during retrieval and workflow validation failures in research and action tasks. The repeated clean runs measured run-to-run variation. Despite using temperature 0 and seed 42, outcomes changed for 49 of 480 instance--paradigm pairs (10.2 percent). Results based on the single reference run are therefore interpreted conservatively, and only clear differences are considered meaningful. The grid informed the first framework version (Section~\ref{subsubsec/inital-Artefact}), while remaining questions motivated the deep-dive experiments in the next section.




\subsubsection{Deep-Dive Task Runs}
\label{subsubsec:TADF/deepdive}

The main grid measured all eight archetypes at all three difficulty levels. Its interpretation, reported in Section~\ref{subsubsec:TADF/evolution}, left five questions open that the archetype design could not separate. Which kinds of plan uncertainty break a pre-planned workflow? Which routing rule wins when two apply at once, and does a strict hierarchy of the task
dimensions hold? Does input volume reduce success or only raise cost? Does a change of the environment during execution require the agent? And can a workflow with compiled conditional branches recover the failures of the
two-stage form? Five task families were designed to isolate these properties. They reused the frozen action-archetype setup so that differences could be attributed to the task rather than the implementation. Each family used the same five clean-run cells as the main grid: two runs on each smaller tier and one on the reference tier. The runs were analyzed to improve the framework. The changes are reported in Section~\ref{subsubsec:TADF/evolution} and the results are in reported in Section~\ref{subsubsec:TADF/results}. 

The first task family P, plan uncertainty, holds 20 instances in four variants. Each variant makes one thing unknown at planning time: the number of actions or the source of a needed fact. To see how theses difrences in uncertency are effecting the outcome. The next task family X, crossed two routing mechanisms from the first framework design. It holds 15 instances in three variants. The first applies codifiable rules to a runtime outcome. The second feeds a two-hop retrieval chain into heterogeneous updates. The third applies a rule matrix that is decidable at planning time but forces a heterogeneous plan. The third variant separates a cascade from a strict hierarchy of dimensions, because the two predict different routes for it. Family L, payload scaling, holds 18 instances in three task shapes: an in-context maximum over cases, a homogeneous
bulk update, and a maximum over a mailbox. Each shape runs at three payload levels of one, five, and twenty times the base volume, with identical instruction templates. The tasks carry no other routing property, so that volume is the only candidate driver. Family M, state drift, holds 15 instances in three variants. Drift is implemented as database triggers that fire on the
run's first write and change a record the run still has to touch. In the observable variant the stale follow-up receives a visible refusal and the runbook names the branch. In the silent variant the stale write succeeds
without a signal. In the guarded variant the runbook instructs a re-check before every modification. Family S, staged adaptivity, tests a third execution form: a state-machine workflow whose branches over the possible
tool outcomes are compiled from a plan and selected deterministically at runtime. It ran on the instances that had produced structural zeros for
the two-stage form and on five new instances that require content synthesized from runtime results.

The five deep-dive families did not directly test one remaining routing element: heterogeneous per-item binding in workflows. The main grid had shown that nine updates with item-specific values broke the workflow's single plan object, whereas sixteen identical updates did not. A map-form workflow addresses this limitation by processing each item in a separate LLM call and dispatching the resulting actions deterministically. A confirmation sweep tested this form on the two original instances. The hypotheses and decision rule were fixed beforehand: the map form should recover the heterogeneous instance at the reference tier without reducing performance on the homogeneous control. A three-way rule then mapped the result to a capability score.

Moreover, two safeguards protected the gold standards for the deep dive families. First, all target sets were computed directly from the seed data and checked through assertions. This rule was introduced after a manually filtered target had caused a gold error. Second, every scoring predicate was tested with positive, negative, and adversarial controls. This helped ensure that the predicates measured the intended state rather than a formatting pattern.

Three apparent findings were traced to measurement artifacts. A cell in which both paradigms scored zero revealed an incorrect gold standard. A workflow failure on mail payloads resulted from the simulated search tool treating quoted queries differently from the real API. A capability inversion in the drift family resulted from a check-then-write race triggered by parallel agent calls. In each case, the error was corrected and the affected cells were rerun. These incidents produced two review rules: cells with zero scores for both paradigms or with capability inversions require manual review, and execution traces take precedence over model self-reports. The mail diagnosis also produced paradigm-relevant design knowledge. Both paradigms initially used the same incorrect query format, but only the agent observed the empty result and reformulated its query. Workflow read stages should therefore check for empty results and permit bounded replanning. Action stages should also reject unresolved placeholders before execution.

\paragraph{Main grid.}
Neither paradigm dominated across all archetypes. The agent achieved higher pooled success in research, structured retrieval, classification, transactional action, and adaptive planning. The workflow performed better in rule-based decisioning, verification, and drafting. The largest differences occurred in transactional action, where the agent led by 26.6 percentage points, and rule-based decisioning, where the workflow led by 20.0 points. Classification was near ceiling for both. Except for verification, the direction of the paradigm difference did not reverse across model tiers. In the high-difficulty verification cell, the workflow trailed at the smallest tier (5/10 versus 7/10), led at the middle tier (9/10 versus 5/10), and held a narrow lead at the reference tier (5/5 versus 4/5). This variation motivated a capability check for every route.

Cost differences were more consistent. The agent required 1.8 to 7.7 times as many tokens per solved run in six archetypes. It was cheaper in classification, while both paradigms were nearly equal in verification. Median latencies differed by only a few seconds, except in research, where the workflow's up-front search batch required 19.1 seconds compared with 6.7 seconds for the agent. Execution errors remained rare, at no more than 4\%. Robustness was higher for the agent in research, retrieval, and action, and higher for the workflow in rule-based decisioning, verification, and planning; both tied in classification and drafting.

Several controlled contrasts explain these aggregate differences. On three outcome-dependent action instances, the workflow solved 0/15 runs and the agent 13/15. In rule-based decisioning, the workflow solved all 75 runs at 903 tokens per run, while the agent solved 60/75; 12 of its 15 failures were permissive under-escalations. On the hardest research tier, success was 56\% for the workflow and 92\% for the agent. For heterogeneous updates, the workflow solved 2/5 runs and the agent 5/5, whereas both solved all homogeneous updates. At the opposite boundary, both paradigms saturated on tasks with six or more simultaneous drafting constraints (1/25 versus 0/25) and difficult cross-system field combinations (8/25 versus 10/25).

The complete variant-level results are reported in Appendix~\ref{app}; the following comparisons focus on the contrasts that changed the framework. Family P showed that different forms of plan uncertainty require different treatment. When one read revealed the required number of actions, both paradigms solved 25/25 runs, while the workflow used only 36\% of the agent's tokens. When the number emerged only from successive action outcomes, the workflow solved 0/25 runs and the agent 15/25. An unknown source location also favored the agent (24/25 versus 12/25). Family X established the priority of runtime feedback over codifiable rules: when a rule depended on an action outcome, the workflow solved 0/25 runs and the agent 24/25. Its third variant revealed a capability interaction. Both paradigms solved 5/5 runs at the reference tier, with the workflow using about 40\% of the agent's tokens; across the smaller tiers, the agent solved 14/20 runs and the workflow 5/20.

Family L found no success cliff within the tested payload range. Across inputs reaching approximately 19,000 tokens for the workflow and 41,000 for the agent, success remained 86/90 and 90/90, respectively. Payload size therefore affected cost but did not act as a correctness gate within this range. Family M produced a sharper boundary. When an external state change generated a visible tool response, the agent solved 25/25 runs and the workflow none. Neither paradigm detected a silent change (0/25 each). When the runbook required a re-check before each modification, the agent recovered 16/25 runs, while the workflow remained at zero. Observable drift therefore favored adaptive control, whereas silent drift required an enforceable system-level safeguard. Family S tested whether a state-machine workflow could replace adaptive control. At the reference tier, precompiled branches recovered both instance sets that had produced structural zeros for the two-stage workflow (5/5 each). Across all tiers, the state machine used approximately one quarter of the agent's tokens on these sets. It did not recover tasks whose output content had to be synthesized from runtime results (4/25 versus 13/25). The results support a distinction between branch selection, which can be compiled when outcomes are enumerable, and runtime synthesis, which requires a separate adaptive stage. The map-form workflow achieved only partial recovery: it solved the heterogeneous instance in two of three runs and the homogeneous control in both runs, at a token cost close to the agent's. The remaining failure omitted three of nine items.

\subsection{The Initial Framework}
\label{subsec:TADF/initial-artifact}
This section presents the decision instrument that carries the measured differences of Section~\ref{subsec:TADF/design} as routing rules. It is the initial artifact of the thesis. Frozen before the evaluation episodes. Section~\ref{subsubsec:TADF/framework} describes the sheet and how a verdict is computed. Section~\ref{subsubsec:TADF/evidence} gives the evidence behind every element. Section~\ref{subsubsec:TADF/functionality} reports the verification of the sheet and its routing value on the corpus. The development trajectory from the first gate cascade to the frozen sheet and the evidence figure of every element are documented in Appendix~\ref{app:evolution}.

\subsubsection{Routing Framework}
\label{subsubsec:TADF/framework} 
The Decision Sheet answers one question for one task at a time: how should this task be executed? Its unit is the task of Section~2.2.3, and its answer is one of four routes: a deterministic implementation withoutan LLM, an LLM workflow, an agent loop, or a decomposition into partsthat re-enter the sheet one by one. Tables~\ref{tab:sheet-gates} and\ref{tab:sheet-stage2} show the instrument. It works in two stages. Stage~1 asks five gate questions in a fixed order. The first questionans wered with yes decides the route, and no later consideration can outweigh it. The first three gates lead out of the paradigm comparison. If predefined deterministic logic can produce the required output, no LLM is needed (Q1). If the task combines several LLM responsibilities(Q2) or exceeds the tested boundary of one reliable execution (Q3), it is decomposed and every part re-enters the sheet. The last two gates fix the agent. If the next step cannot be predefined but must be chosenby the model at runtime, the task needs the agent loop (Q4). The sameholds if the task requires an adaptive search over an open or unknown space (Q5). A task that passes all five gates reaches Stage~2.

Stage~2 compares the two paradigms by points. Nine criteria enter the comparison. The three task-fit criteria apply only when their condition holds and then enter at the fixed weight of 3. The six deployment indicators always apply; the architect sets their weight from 0 to 3 forthe deployment context, or marks an indicator as mandatory (M). Every criterion carries a fixed capability score for each paradigm, from 1 to 3, that the evidence of Section~\ref{subsubsec:TADF/evidence} sets and the architect does not change. The score of a paradigm is the sum, over all applying criteria, of weight times capability. The higher score routes, and the workflow routes on ties. M is non-compensable: it counts asweight 3 in the sum and excludes the paradigm whose capability on thatindicator is 1, regardless of the totals. M works in both directions,for example safe failure against the agent and evolvability against theworkflow.

Two worked examples show the arithmetic. The first is a quote-approvaltask on which rule success (T1) applies. The deployment weights safe failure at 3, traceability at 2, and the other indicators at 1. The agent scores $3 \cdot 2 = 6$ on T1 and $1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 +3 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 3 = 16$ on the indicators, 22in total. The task routes to the workflow. The second is a bulk update whose items carry different values from unstructured text, so extraction success (T2) applies. The deployment weights robustness and evolvability at 3 and the other indicators at 1. The workflow scores $3\cdot 2 = 6$ on T2 and $3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 1 = 21$ on the indicators, 27 in total. The agent scores $3 \cdot 3 = 9$ on T2 and $1 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 3 \cdot 3 + 3 \cdot 3 = 24$ on the indicators, 33 intotal. The task routes to the agent. One row of Table~\ref{tab:sheet-stage2} carries a caveat that the write-up established. The scores of the retrieval criterion T3 rest on adevelopment run of the second implementation state of the retrieval archetype, which the final grid did not reproduce (Section~\ref{subsubsec:TADF/results}). The criterion is reported here as frozen and retired in Section~\ref{subsec:TADF/artifact}. 

The sheet is the first of three layers. The second layer attaches the design knowledge that a verdict needs to become a build. It holds fourworkflow build forms: the canonical two-stage form (W1), the state machinewith compiled conditional edges (W2), the rule engine fed by LLM-extractedfacts (W3), and the map form with one small LLM call per item (W4). It holds ten build rules (B1 to B10). They range from per-item callsand a post-action synthesis stage over read-stage emptiness checks, placeholder guards,schema and revision budgets, agent iteration budgets, and post-statevalidation to the mandate for atomic and idempotent writes and thehandling of paradigm-typed failures. And it holds six capability checks(C1 to C6) that name the model tier from which a form is reliable. Thethird layer records the evidence behind every row of the sheet: the cells,the numbers, and the development-log entries that set each score. A conceptualdecision model re-expresses the sheet for communication. Its four decisionsteps mirror the sheet's blocks, subtask qualification (Q1 to Q3), agentnecessity (Q4, Q5), evidence-based task fit (T1 to T3), and deploymentpreferences (P1 to P6), embedded in the four knowledge stores that thearchitect consults: task context, tool landscape, system architecture, anddeployment context. The model carries no capability numbers; those live inthe sheet and its rulebook.

The sheet is the ninth version of the framework. The interpretation ofthe main grid produced a gate cascade that an architect walks top tobottom (v1). The deep-dive families resolved its open cells and refuted astrict hierarchy of task dimensions (v1.1, v1.2). A two-stage instrument,a structured self-review, the three layers, and a mandatory-constrainedfit matrix followed (v1.3 to v2.1). The scoring rulebook and theconfirmation sweep then fixed every number, and the sheet was frozen(v2.2). Appendix~\ref{app:evolution} documents every version with itstrigger, its change, and its evidence.

\subsubsection{Evidence}\label{subsubsec:TADF/evidence}Every element of the sheet rests on a measured difference ofSection~\ref{subsubsec:TADF/results}, and every capability number derivesfrom a written rule. This section gives the rule first and then theevidence element by element. Appendix~\ref{app:evolution} shows onefigure per element, generated from the master file with a drift guardthat asserts every plotted value against its declaration.

\paragraph{The scoring rulebook.}Every capability number derives from a rule instead of judgment.Success bands on the same pooled basis for both paradigms set the task-fitscores: at least 90\% gives 3, 50 to 89\% gives 2, below 50\% gives 1. Theband width exceeds the 10.2\% flip noise. An admission rule scores atask-fit criterion only if the paradigms differ by at least one band on thesame basis. Band-equal structures become build knowledge instead of scores.Cost bands over the region the comparison governs set the efficiencyscore: up to 1.5 times the cheaper paradigm gives 3, up to 3 times gives 2,more gives 1. The rulebook declared the agent's median premium as 3.6 timesthe workflow's tokens per solved task, which yields 1. This median did notreproduce from the per-archetype ratios and was restated after the freeze(Section~\ref{subsec:TADF/evaluation}). An error-direction rule scores theparadigm whose failure direction is a design choice at 3 and the paradigmwith uncontrolled, majority-dangerous failures at 1. Traceability and theschema guarantee follow a construction rubric: guaranteed by construction3, achievable through an additional control 2, not controllable 1.Robustness is scored by the band rule on input-surface noise only. Acount-once rule prevents the same measured effect from entering as a gateand again as a score. Evolvability is the only score without ameasurement; it is derived from the migration path of a changing task andmarked as such.

\begin{description}  \item[Q3, granularity.] Some cells saturate for both paradigms and mark  the boundary of one execution: six or more simultaneous drafting  constraints scored 1 of 25 against 0 of 25, cross-system field  combination in retrieval 8 of 25 against 10 of 25, and the hardest  action tier 0 of 5 for both. The gate therefore recommends decomposition  rather than a paradigm.  \item[Q4, adaptive control.] Wherever the next step depends on the  outcome of a preceding action, on an observable state change, or on a  mandated re-check, the two-stage workflow scores zero and the agent  carries the task: 0 of 15 against 13 of 15 on the runtime-feedback  instances, 1 of 5 against 5 of 5 on the read-conditional instance, 0 of  25 against 25 of 25 on observable drift, and 0 of 25 against 16 of 25 on  guarded drift. The result is invariant across tiers.  \item[Q5, adaptive search.] Search that must build on its own  intermediate results, or whose source is unknown, favors the agent at  every tier: 56\% against 92\% on the hardest research tier and 12 of 25  against 24 of 25 on the unknown-source variant. Low-stakes lookups are  equal and cheaper in the workflow.  \item[T1, rule success.] Rules that can be fetched at runtime and  applied by code run without error in the workflow, 75 of 75 at 903  tokens per run, while the agent under-escalates, 60 of 75 with 12 of its  15 errors permissive. The workflow scores 3, the agent 2.  \item[T2, extraction success.] Item-specific values across a bulk  operation break one combined plan object (2 of 5 against 5 of 5) and  are only partially recovered by the map form (2 of 3), while identical  values are no criterion (5 of 5 both). The workflow scores 2, the agent  3, measured on both sides in the confirmation sweep.  \item[T3, retrieval success.] Declared on the development basis of the  second retrieval state (15 of 15 against 6 of 15); not reproduced by the  grid (49 of 75 against 58 of 75, band-equal), retired before submission.  \item[P1, efficiency.] Cost is paradigm-typed. Per solved task the  agent spends 1.8 to 7.7 times the workflow's tokens in six archetypes;  classification is inverted (0.8) and verification at parity  (Table~\ref{tab:grid-results}). The workflow scores 3, the agent 1.  \item[P2 and P4, traceability and schema guarantee.] Traces and  schema-bound output exist by construction in the workflow and post hoc  in the agent. Both follow the construction rubric: workflow 3, agent 2.  \item[P3, safe failure direction.] The agent's failures concentrate on  the dangerous side: 12 of its 15 rule-application errors were silent  approvals. The workflow scores 3, the agent 1.  \item[P5, robustness to messy input.] The agent tolerates messy  phrasing better on retrieval and action (15 of 15 against 13 of 15 on  the retrieval suite), the workflow on compliance (18 of 18 against 13 of  18), which is noted as counter-evidence. The workflow scores 2, the  agent 3.  \item[P6, evolvability.] Not measured. The score is derived from the  migration path of a changing task definition and marked as derived,  reserved for the practitioner episode.  \item[Default.] Where no gate fires, the agent's premium buys no  additional success: rule application and drafting are workflow-led,  classification is at ceiling for both, and identical bulk updates score  5 of 5 for both. The sheet therefore defaults to the workflow.\end{description}

Two agent-side elements stand out. Extraction success is the only task-fitcriterion the agent leads, measured on both sides after the confirmationsweep. Robustness to messy input is the only indicator the agent leads.Both rest on the same mechanism, continuous self-observation, that alsoproduces the two agent gates.

\subsubsection{Functionality and Routing Value}
\label{subsubsec:TADF/functionality}Mechanical checks passed before the freeze. The sheet resolves all 256combinations of its inputs, gate order does not change any verdict, andneutral profiles are equivalent on 19 reference cases. The rulebook wasbacktested against the full experimental corpus on the bases declared inthe rulebook. All 30 task groups route correctly, 28 on measured cellsand 2 on design-tagged derivations whose confirmation sweeps are named asopen. On the final grid basis the retrieval group is band-equal, so itsworkflow route stands as the default rather than as a scored verdict.After the freeze, one figure per sheet element was generated with a driftguard: every grid value is declared once, re-derived from the raw masterfile, and asserted against its declaration. 44 values were checked withzero mismatches (Appendix~\ref{app:evolution}). The scorecard made oneasymmetry visible: the workflow leads six rows and the agent two.

The value of the routing rules on the corpus was quantified against naivebaselines. Figure~\ref{fig:routing-value} places six routing strategieson the same task groups by success and by cost. The comparison covers the17 measured task groups with a success pair for both paradigms on the samebasis, 710 instance-runs in total, with the main-grid archetypes at theirpooled grid values; cost is the total token spend of a strategy divided byits solved runs. Routed by the frozen Decision Sheet, the groups reach82.5\% success, the same share as always-agent, against 85.5\% for theper-group oracle, 74.9\% for random routing, and 67.2\% foralways-workflow. The routed portfolio spends 6,832 tokens per solved task,always-agent 12,851, the oracle 10,822, and always-workflow 4,788. Therouter thus matches the agent's success at about half of its cost,because it takes the workflow in every group where no gate fires, andthere the workflow is the cheaper side in all groups except classificationand verification. The gap to the oracle consists of five groups routed tothe workflow on purpose, four of them band-equal. The comparison isin-sample, because the rules were derived from the same data; it is aconsistency check, not an evaluation. The out-of-sample test is Episode~2(Section~\ref{subsec:TADF/evaluation}), and the strategy totals are listedin Appendix~\ref{app:results}.
